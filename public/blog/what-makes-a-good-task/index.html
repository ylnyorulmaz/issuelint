What is a task—and what makes a good one?

A vague ticket can consume more time than a difficult one. Before anyone codes, designs or delivers, there is a more basic question: is the work clear enough to start without inventing requirements?

IssueLint Editorial · November 22, 2026 · Task quality
IN THIS ARTICLE DefinitionThe essentialsWhy eleven checks?All 11 checksExamplesSources

What is a task?

A task is a specific piece of work, investigation or decision someone can act on and recognize as complete. It might be a Jira ticket, a Linear issue, an item in a to-do list, a marketing brief, a bug report or a request handed to an AI coding agent. What matters is that the next person understands the action and its boundary.

OBSERVATION“Checkout feels slow.”

Worth noting, but not yet an instruction to execute.

GOAL“Improve checkout.”

A useful direction, but many incompatible changes could satisfy it.

TASK“On mobile checkout, show a loading state after Place order until payment succeeds or fails.”

A bounded behavior change that can be reviewed.

This distinction echoes Exploring the Black Box: capturing ideas and clarifying tasks are different activities. Moving a note into a project-management system does not automatically make it actionable.

The essentials of a good task

Our practical definition is simple: a good task is understandable, actionable, appropriately scoped and tied to a defined result. It gives a capable colleague enough information to begin and makes it possible to recognize when the work is finished.

OptimEdge's guide emphasizes action-led titles and descriptions containing context and the done state. Alex Kitchens adds practical advice about manageable subtasks, relevant links and tasks you can actually complete. These are useful principles, not a demand for elaborate documentation.

A one-line button rename can be a complete task. A cross-service migration may need a detailed specification, approvals and tests. The right amount of detail depends on the cost of ambiguity, not on the number of empty fields in a template.

How we arrived at eleven checks

“Be specific” is sensible advice, but too broad to consistently guide a review. IssueLint turns recurring questions from task handoffs into eleven distinct dimensions: conflicting instructions, readiness to act, boundaries, size, behavior, completion, outcome, references, prerequisites, wording and real implementation obstacles.

We use these dimensions because the fixes are different. A missing design link calls for a reference; an impossible timing constraint calls for a decision; an oversized assignment may need splitting. Treating them all as generic “clarity issues” produces weak feedback and duplicate questions.

Traditional task-writing guidance helps explain the emphasis on actions, context and completion. Another useful lens comes from HUD's “What is a good task?”. HUD studies AI-agent training and evaluation tasks comprising a prompt, environment and grader, and emphasizes realism, fairness and difficulty. Its warning that a grader should judge actual correctness—not superficial keywords—helps motivate careful evidence and completion checks.

Important distinction. HUD is describing benchmark design, not ordinary project tickets. A day-to-day work request does not need to be deliberately difficult. And none of these sources publishes IssueLint's exact eleven-category rubric. This rubric is our own practical engineering synthesis, not a universal or scientifically validated standard. Its ability to catch important problems still needs testing with real user tasks.

THE ISSUE/LINT FRAMEWORK

Eleven quality checks—and why they matter

IssueLint currently checks the following dimensions. Each is a question about a potential decision or handoff problem, not a mandatory form field. Checks that do not apply should not be treated as failures.

  1. 01

    Contradictions

    Can all the instructions be satisfied together?

    A request that says “email everyone today” and “wait for tomorrow's approval” contains incompatible directions. The useful output is a question about priority, not a confident guess.

    Guardrail: An apparent tension is not automatically a contradiction; only material conflicts justify a flag.

  2. 02

    Actionability

    Can the assignee start meaningful work?

    “Improve onboarding” is a topic, not a next action. “Draft the onboarding email using the approved welcome copy” gives a clear starting point.

    Guardrail: Actionable does not mean specifying every implementation step.

  3. 03

    Scope

    Are the relevant boundaries understood?

    “Add dark mode” could mean one Settings screen, an entire web app or both mobile platforms. Define the included surfaces when they change what will be built.

    Guardrail: A tiny, unambiguous edit does not need a long in-scope/out-of-scope section.

  4. 04

    Granularity

    Is the work one cohesive, manageable unit?

    A task mixing a billing migration, a dashboard redesign and a campaign launch may be three separately deliverable projects. Breaking them up may improve ownership and review.

    Guardrail: Several tightly coupled steps can still form one sensible task.

  5. 05

    Behavior

    Is the expected behavior defined?

    A theme toggle leaves decisions about manual overrides, system defaults and persistence. These are product behaviors, not coding trivia.

    Guardrail: Tasks that change only copy or documentation may have no behavior question at all.

  6. 06

    Completion

    How will we tell when the task is done?

    “Improve the dashboard” has an uncertain end state. “Rename the Settings button from Submit to Save changes” is plainly complete when the intended label appears.

    Guardrail: Do not require numeric KPIs or elaborate acceptance tests for a small obvious change.

  7. 07

    Outcome

    What result is this meant to create?

    “Send a report” describes activity. “Send the weekly revenue summary to finance for Monday's review” clarifies the intended deliverable and its use.

    Guardrail: An atomic task may convey its outcome through the title alone.

  8. 08

    References

    Can the assignee locate required source material?

    “Implement the approved Figma” is blocked if the mandatory design is not supplied or locatable. A reference is useful only if it leads to the actual artifact.

    Guardrail: No linked document is necessary when the task does not depend on one.

  9. 09

    Dependencies

    What has to happen first?

    Legal approval, access to a staging environment or another team's API may be a real prerequisite. Name the blocker and its status if it affects the work.

    Guardrail: Do not invent approvals merely to fill a template.

  10. 10

    Clarity

    Could two capable readers reasonably interpret this differently?

    “Make it modern” and “improve performance” may conceal different meanings. Specify the observable change that matters to this request.

    Guardrail: Short wording is not inherently unclear.

  11. 11

    Feasibility

    Is there a separately supported obstacle to doing the work?

    If a task explicitly requires a capability the environment lacks, that can be a blocker worth raising before delivery begins.

    Guardrail: Uncertainty about implementation effort is not evidence that a request is impossible.

Three tasks, three different decisions

01 / TOO VAGUE

“Add dark mode to the app.”

The missing decisions: Which platforms or screens? Should the system theme be used? Is the user's selection persisted? Is there an existing design reference?

A clearer version: “Add a Light/Dark toggle to the web app's Settings screen only. Default to the system preference unless the user has selected a theme; persist their choice across reloads. Mobile is out of scope.”

That resolves scope, behavior and completion without inventing a deadline or a KPI.

02 / CONTRADICTORY

“Email every lead today, but wait for tomorrow's approval.”

This contains an apparent conflict between sending today and requiring approval tomorrow. The correct next step is to ask which condition has priority, not to silently rewrite either requirement. Sometimes a clarification question is the best output.

03 / ALREADY GOOD

“On /settings, rename ‘Submit’ to ‘Save changes’. Do not change behavior.”

The location, exact change and scope are clear. Requiring explicit business KPIs, a Figma file or a ten-point acceptance checklist would add bureaucracy without removing risk.

A five-minute check before work begins

  1. Start with an action or a question. Name the deliverable or the decision you need.
  2. Identify the material unknowns. What could cause two competent people to deliver different things?
  3. Check scope and completion. Where does the change apply, and what shows that it is finished?
  4. Resolve contradictions and dependencies. Do not make unauthorized product or business decisions for the author.
  5. Skip irrelevant paperwork. A tiny, self-contained change does not need every field.
